## Academic Biography

# Academic Narrative CV: Professor Simone Di Leo

Simone Di Leo's scholarly identity is anchored in the methodological problem of how to produce robust, decision-relevant evidence from complex heterogeneous data, whether those data describe universities, municipal waste systems, photovoltaic investment portfolios, or the rapidly expanding universe of scholarly publications themselves. His research program is animated by a recurring tension between the demand for comparable, multidimensional performance assessment and the equally pressing demand for context-sensitivity, fairness across units, and integrity of the underlying information base. Across the trajectory that runs from frontier efficiency benchmarking to the techno-economic appraisal of renewable energy systems, Di Leo has consistently insisted that measurement instruments, be they Data Envelopment Analysis variants, composite SDG indicators, or systematic literature reviews, must be evaluated by their consequences for the decision-maker rather than by their mathematical elegance in isolation. This commitment unites work that might otherwise appear scattered across operations research, energy economics, and research-infrastructure science, and it is this commitment that organises the thematic arcs that follow.

## Frontier Efficiency Benchmarks as Epistemic Instruments

The intellectual centre of gravity of Di Leo's work lies in the development of frontier benchmarking frameworks in which Data Envelopment Analysis is not deployed as a black-box ranking device but as a structured conversation about which indicators belong on which dimension of performance, and for whom the comparison is fair. In 'DEA, balanced scorecard and intellectual capital including the gender dimension: A comprehensive list of indicators' (2023), Di Leo and co-authors operationalise a three-level methodology that fuses systematic review, bibliometric mapping, and grey-literature mining to recover an inclusive list of intangible-capital indicators, reclassifying them under the canonical dimensions of human, structural, and relational capital and embedding the gender dimension as a first-class contextual variable. The result is an indicator architecture in which the choice of variables, the choice of algorithm, and the choice of data source are simultaneously problematised. That problematisation is the subject of 'Meta-choices in ranking knowledge-based organizations' (2022), which treats quantification and measurement not as neutral technical exercises but as epistemological acts that shape managerial satisficing in knowledge-based organisations, drawing on the heuristic of the technologies of humility to argue that benchmarking conclusions must be reported with explicit awareness of the implementation problem.

These commitments carry directly into the study of universities as knowledge-intensive DMUs. In 'Heterogeneity of national accounting systems, world-class universities and financial resources: What are the links?' (2024), Di Leo and colleagues show that the position of a university in the highest tier of the Shanghai ranking is tightly coupled to the financial resources at its disposal, but only conditional on a critical observation that financial data are themselves constructed through heterogeneous national accounting systems that render cross-country comparison anything but straightforward. The regulatory-economics counterpart of this stance emerges in 'The role of the non-academic staff heterogeneity in the performance of European universities' (2026), in which unobserved heterogeneity of administrative and technical personnel is recovered through a nonparametric latent-factor approach on a sample of 401 European universities, allowing the authors to separate bureaucratic overload from productive heterogeneity. Building on this diagnostic infrastructure, 'Climbing university rankings under resources constraints: a combined approach integrating DEA and directed Louvain community detection' (2025) proposes that universities should not be benchmarked against an unconditional global frontier but against peers with similar financial profiles, identified through Louvain community detection, so that the targets offered to resource-constrained institutions remain realistic. The paper extends the frontier-benchmarking tradition with a heuristic and network-theoretic apparatus applied to the Italian university case. A complementary heuristic angle is developed in 'A heuristic approach based on Leiden rankings to identify outliers: evidence from Italian universities in the European landscape' (2023), where Leiden Rankings are repurposed not as a definitive ordering but as a statistical and network substrate for surfacing outlier institutions whose performance merits case-study scrutiny.

The same frontier logic is transported, with the same emphasis on conditionality, into the waste sector. In 'Driving EU sustainability: Promoting the circular economy through municipal waste efficiency' (2024), Di Leo applies standard DEA to twenty-seven European countries between 2017 and 2021 across three optimisation models (economic, technical, and sustainability), surfacing fragmented performance and the strategic role of technology in minimising landfilling. The regulatory counterpart is developed in 'Impact of a regulatory target and external factors on the waste efficiency of Italian municipalities' (2025), in which the binding 65 percent sorted-collection target of Legislative Decree 152/2006 is treated as an exogenous constraint that interacts with population and urban economic development in shaping municipal cost efficiency, motivating a neighbourhood-solidarity policy. At a finer spatial grain, 'Viable eco-efficiency targets for waste collection communities' (2024) introduces conditional efficiency analysis for the ATO municipalities of the Città Metropolitana di Roma Capitale, embedding fairness and generalisability constraints into the efficient frontier itself so that eco-efficiency targets are not only optimal but viable given community-specific exogenous context. At the European macro level, 'A Flexible and Sustainable Analysis of Waste Efficiency at the European Level' (2024) complements this conditional-DEA tradition with directional distance functions that treat landfilling and incineration as bad outputs and classify EU countries into five performance tiers in order to derive realistic sustainability targets.

The same problem of how to make the boundary of a comparison set methodologically defensible recurs in the energy-retail domain. 'A Framework for the Analysis of the Sustainability of the Energy Retail Market' (2022) builds, through a systematic review complemented by bibliometric mapping of forty-eight papers, a conceptual framework for sustainability assessment that distinguishes technical, economic, and environmental impacts and their interaction effects. The framework is operationalised in 'A robust benchmarking of direct margin in Italy's energy retail markets' (2024), where the post-2000 liberalisation and the 2016 bifurcation between retail and other activities in Italy are exploited to construct a robust direct-margin benchmarking of one hundred and twenty retailers, yielding a positive size–efficiency gradient and identifying commercial-operational differentiation as a second-order driver. Together these studies show that Di Leo's frontier-benchmarking work is not a series of case studies but a single methodological project: to make benchmarking honest about its own boundary choices.

## Integrating Photovoltaics, Storage, and Policy into a Common Profitability Grammar

A second arc traces Di Leo's engagement with the techno-economic assessment of photovoltaic and battery energy storage systems, where Monte Carlo simulation, LASSO regression, and NPV analysis are welded into a single analytical grammar in which policy regimes, self-consumption behaviour, and avoided CO2 valuation jointly determine investment viability. In 'Green restaurants: An economic assessment of solar photovoltaics and energy storage systems' (2026), the catering sector is treated as an energy-intensive context in which the viability of PV and BES depends critically on the alignment of public incentives with self-consumption strategy; the authors report that PV profitability ranges from 425 to 1590 €/kW across policy scenarios and that BES profitability is contingent on a 22–25 percent increase in self-consumption under incentives and 30–35 percent without them. The industrial analogue appears in 'Economic assessment of battery energy storage in industrial applications: Policy support and self-consumption effects' (2026), which evaluates a 1500 kWh BES integrated with a 1 MW PV plant at a pharmaceutical site and shows, through Monte Carlo simulation, that demand-management self-consumption has a stronger effect on profitability than equivalent CAPEX reductions, with break-even self-consumption decreasing from 44 percent without grants to 31 percent under favourable purchase prices.

These case studies are absorbed into a policy-oriented framework in 'A policy-oriented framework for assessing the economic sustainability of industrial photovoltaic investments' (2026), which formalises industrial PV evaluation through discounted cash flow, scenario analysis, break-even assessment, and Monte Carlo simulation and reports a 45 percent capital grant lifting NPV from 1190 €/kW to 1610 €/kW while monetising avoided CO2 emissions between 37,000 and 63,000 €. The same quantitative framework is extended to commercial buildings in 'Advancing Sustainable Development Through Integrated Photovoltaic and Battery Energy Storage Systems in Commercial Buildings' (2025), where a strategic expert survey recommends a 50 percent tax deduction and BES economic viability is shown to depend strongly on market scenario and avoided electricity costs, with a first-year emission reduction of 77 tCO2eq from an integrated system. The framework is further articulated in 'Towards SDG 7: A Quantitative Sustainability Framework for Evaluating PV+BES Systems in the Low-Carbon Transition' (2026), where LASSO regression identifies the energy purchase price and self-consumption rate as the dominant levers across PV, BES, and avoided-emission components in a Rome shopping-mall case.

The behavioural counterpart of this techno-economic agenda is developed in 'Energy policy and citizens' perceptions: The role of PV+BES systems in driving a sustainable energy transition' (2026), where survey evidence is used to identify the demographic clusters of citizens most receptive to PV+BES investment, to document the perceived necessity of BES for households unable to modify consumption habits, and to position self-consumption, rather than government incentives, as the most valued attribute. The arc closes at the macro-financial level with 'Exploring the economic feasibility of offshore wind energy for sustainable development' (2025), which treats offshore wind deployment as an NPV-distribution problem rather than a pure technology assessment and shows that the central-Italian offshore case ranges from 195 to 1324 €/kW across capacity-factor, inflation, and selling-price scenarios. Read together, these papers constitute a sustained effort to construct a common economic grammar in which policy regimes, end-user behaviour, and environmental externalities are jointly quantified, and in which the same Monte Carlo, LASSO, and NPV machinery is portable across catering, industry, commerce, residential demand, and offshore generation.

## The Information Infrastructure Beneath the Benchmarks

Underneath both the efficiency-benchmarking and the energy-economics strands runs a third arc in which Di Leo interrogates the data infrastructure on which empirical analysis depends. In 'An integrated dataset providing a landscape of the Italian Universities' (2023), heterogeneous Italian higher-education sources — USTAT, balance-sheet records, and Scopus metadata — are harmonised into a unified structural-and-economic dataset that researchers can use for downstream econometric analysis, embodying the data-engineering stance that research requires cross-source landscapes before measurement. A complementary infrastructure concern motivates 'Accounting for quality in data integration systems: a completeness-aware integration approach' (2022), which treats multi-national, multi-temporal data fusion as a completeness-aware quality problem and argues that integration systems should be evaluated by user-side data quality rather than by the accuracy of individual sources, extending the role of data infrastructures towards knowledge infrastructures through a case study in research infrastructure for science and innovation studies. The same diagnostic instinct is sharpened at the level of individual time series in 'Flexible Techniques to Detect Typical Hidden Errors in Large Longitudinal Datasets' (2024), where a context-independent indicator system exploits the symmetries and asymmetries of consecutive differences to surface elusive residual errors in the ETER database after generic correction techniques have run, formalising hidden-error detection as an anomaly problem on large longitudinal panels.

## Methodological Self-Reflection: Systematic Reviews as Metaknowledge

A fourth, reflexive arc treats the systematic literature review itself as a methodological object, both as a producer of metaknowledge and as a site of AI-assisted transformation. In 'Systematic reviews as a metaknowledge tool: caveats and a review of available options' (2023), Di Leo and co-authors catalogue the rapidly expanding toolkit of computer-assisted review methods, contrast systematic review with domain-expert-based selection through a content analysis of International Transactions in Operational Research, and argue that good review practice is the intelligent combination of available tools with expert judgement rather than the substitution of one for the other. The same journal functions as the empirical site in 'Pitfalls, benefits, and comparative analysis of artificial intelligence ChatBots in the systematic review process' (2026), which compares AI ChatBot performance against manual execution across SLR stages, document practical implications and pitfalls, and identifies the transformative potential of AI for information retrieval and knowledge synthesis while cautioning against overreliance. Together these studies frame the SLR not as a transparent pipeline but as a methodological choice problem — the very kind of meta-choice that 'Meta-choices in ranking knowledge-based organizations' (2022) identifies at the heart of multidimensional benchmarking, and which now returns at the meta-level of how scholarship itself is filtered.

## Comparative Sustainability, Consumer Behaviour, and Digital Ecosystems

Two further arcs extend the program's reach into comparative sustainability assessment and into the consumer- and ecosystem-level determinants of circular-economy outcomes. In 'Evaluating sustainability in Europe with composite indicators' (2025), Di Leo and co-authors normalise seventy-six Eurostat SDG indicators for 2022 through min–max and rank EU countries via TOPSIS under equal weighting, demonstrating strong consistency between the two normalisation schemes and recommending cohesion policies to reduce sustainability disparities. At the consumer level, 'From Awareness to Action: Consumer Behaviour, Attitudes and Business Strategy in Sustainable and Circular Electronic Devices' (2026) uses k-means clustering, principal component analysis, and statistical tests on a 407-respondent survey to segment Italian consumers along attitudinal and generational lines, recovering the gap between environmental awareness and consistent disposal behaviour and identifying differentiated strategies for younger, resource-constrained, and eco-conscious segments. The same segmentation logic is repurposed at the ecosystem level in 'Integrating knowledge management and digital product passports to foster sustainable and collaborative ecosystems' (2026), where the Digital Product Passport is theorised as a socio-technical infrastructure coupling open innovation and knowledge management with circular-economy practice in the Italian fashion and cosmetics sectors, and where the success of DPP implementation is shown to depend on the joint alignment of organisational readiness with aware and unaware consumer segments.

## Convexity, Cones, and the Limits of Standard Frontiers

Distinct from but intellectually adjacent to the applied benchmarking work, a technical-methodological arc centres on the measurement of directional distances and Luenberger productivity indices when the attainable set violates convexity. In 'Conical Free Disposal Hull estimators of directional distances and Luenberger productivity indices for general technologies' (2025), Di Leo and co-authors introduce estimators of directional distances to the cone spanned by the attainable set, derive their statistical properties, and enable Luenberger productivity measurement and decomposition under Constant Returns to Scale without imposing convexity, illustrating the inferential procedure on simulated and well-known real data. This strand functions as the formal substrate of the applied work: every time the program relaxes convexity, conditions on external factors, or builds directed distance functions, the methodological warrant can be traced back to this technical arc.

## Synthesis and Intellectual Horizon

Across these arcs, Di Leo's research program displays a coherent methodological identity in which the construction of measurement instruments, the construction of the data on which they operate, and the construction of the comparisons they enable are treated as a single integrated problem. The frontier-benchmarking tradition conditions efficiency and ranking analysis on financial profiles, on unobserved staff heterogeneity, on community context, and on regulatory targets, replacing unconditional global comparisons with benchmarks that are realistic, viable, and fair. The PV+BES and offshore-wind agenda builds a portable grammar in which NPV, Monte Carlo, LASSO, and avoided-CO2 valuation allow policy regimes, self-consumption behaviour, and environmental externalities to be analysed jointly across catering, industrial, commercial, residential, and offshore contexts. The data-infrastructure strand ensures that both agendas rest on a quality-assured empirical foundation, from the integration of Italian university records to the completeness-aware logic of multinational data fusion and the indicator-driven detection of hidden errors in longitudinal panels. The methodological self-reflection on systematic reviews ties all of this together by treating the SLR, like the benchmark, as a meta-choice problem that combines tool-assisted automation with expert judgement. Read together, these strands position Di Leo's forward trajectory at the intersection of three evolving scientific demands: the demand for benchmarking protocols that remain credible under heterogeneity, the demand for renewable-energy appraisal frameworks that are portable across sectors and policy regimes, and the demand for research-infrastructures and review methodologies that are transparent about their own quality assumptions. The work that remains, on the evidence of the existing program, is to deepen the coupling between these demands: to import the conditional, fairness-aware logic of the waste and university benchmarks into the energy and digital-ecosystem appraisals, and to anchor the SLR and AI-assisted review experiments within the same quality-aware data infrastructures that already underlie the empirical benchmarking program.
