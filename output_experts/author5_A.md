## Rigorous Transport Economics at the Intersection of Decarbonization and Public Policy

Professor Mirko Giagnorio has established a distinguished research program at the convergence of transport economics, industrial organization, operations research, and empirical policy design. His scholarly inquiry addresses a central imperative of modern public infrastructure: how public authorities and private operators can navigate the multi-dimensional transition toward decarbonized transit systems while ensuring cost efficiency, regulatory fairness, and social welfare optimization. Through a synthesis of microeconomic theory, engineering-based bottom-up modeling, interpretable machine learning, and choice experiments, Professor Giagnorio constructs normative and empirical frameworks that reshape how public transport concessions are benchmarked, tendered, and operated.

At the core of Professor Giagnorio’s program is a methodological commitment to replacing opaque historical cost benchmarks with granular, structural models capable of isolating genuine operational efficiency from exogenous market conditions and technological uncertainties. By bridging the gap between bottom-up engineering production functions and high-level microeconomic welfare economics, his research provides actionable decision-support tools for public transport authorities, fleet operators, and energy regulators. This integrated approach allows for a rigorous assessment of alternative powertrains, infrastructure market designs, and tender compensation mechanisms under evolving economic and technological paradigms.

## Engineering-Grounded Standard Cost Modeling for Contracted Rail Systems

To overcome the distortions inherent in historical cost accounting for public transit concessions, Professor Giagnorio pioneered parametric and hybrid benchmarking models designed to establish normative unit costs and set maximum economic compensation bases in competitive tendering. In 'A proxy cost model for tramway services' (2020), he developed a structural cost decomposition framework separating transport service production, infrastructure, maintenance, administrative, and capital expenditures using complete empirical data from Italian tramway operators. Through detailed simulation protocols, this work isolated the marginal impacts of cost drivers under firm control, such as driver and rolling stock productivity, against those dictated by local authorities, including fleet age and contracted vehicle revenue kilometers.

Expanding this parametric approach to higher-capacity urban rail, Professor Giagnorio formulated a comprehensive benchmarking methodology in 'A hybrid cost model for light and heavy metro services' (2025). By accounting for critical engineering variables such as peak hourly capacity, automation levels, and wheel technology across Italian metro networks, the study revealed structural cost divergences between light and heavy metro systems. The findings demonstrated that heavy metro services exhibit higher capital intensity and train depreciation per kilometer, yet achieve superior cost-efficiency per seat-kilometer when deployed under high-demand conditions. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses showed that infrastructure maintenance gains yield greater cost-reduction leverage than rolling stock maintenance, offering clear guidelines for public authorities negotiating concession contracts or structuring competitive auctions.

## Microeconomic Welfare Optimization and Systemic Fleet Electrification

Building upon structural cost models for fixed-guideway transit, Professor Giagnorio extended his analytical framework to integrate microeconomic welfare optimization with bottom-up engineering production functions in urban and regional transport. In 'A bottom-up cost model for electric railbased regional public transport services: The ITALIAN CONTEXT' (2021), he constructed a standardized engineering cost model for electric regional rail that limits the influence of historical inefficiencies. Calibrated on operational data covering ninety-five percent of the Italian market, the model demonstrated that commercial speed and service frequency serve as the primary determinants of standard unit costs, providing public authorities with precise parameters to calculate baseline compensation across varying route stop profiles.

This engineering-economic synthesis directly informed his work on road transit decarbonization in 'Introducing electric buses in urban areas: Effects on welfare, pricing, frequency, and public subsidies' (2024). Examining high-frequency urban bus corridors such as those in Stockholm, Professor Giagnorio modeled the systemic welfare trade-offs between opportunity charging and depot charging fleet configurations. The study established that while electric buses substantially reduce local health-damaging and carbon emissions, high upfront battery and charging infrastructure costs can prevent them from being immediately social-welfare-improving without concurrent structural policy interventions. By optimizing transit fares, service frequencies, and urban road pricing simultaneously, he proved that operational efficiency gains—achieved through optimized climate control, driving behavior, and route planning—can match or exceed the cost benefits of raw capital cost reductions in battery technology.

## Techno-Economic Life-Cycle Performance Across Heterogeneous Transit Operational Contexts

Recognizing that the financial and environmental viability of alternative powertrains depends heavily on spatial and operational constraints, Professor Giagnorio developed empirical life-cycle cost frameworks grounded in extensive fleet data. In 'Assessing cost-effectiveness of alternative bus technologies: Evidence from US transit agencies' (2023), he formulated a life-cycle cost model capturing real-world operational variables across 256 transport operators representing over eighty percent of urban bus revenue miles in the United States. This empirical investigation demonstrated how service parameters—specifically commercial speed, route congestion, and annual mileage—dictate technology competitiveness.

The findings established that full-electric buses achieve clear economic and environmental cost-competitiveness in dense metropolises characterized by severe congestion, high service frequency, and high marginal damages from urban air pollution. Conversely, in suburban and rural settings marked by longer, faster routes, electric transit technologies face persistent economic and operational hurdles relative to conventional or hybrid powertrains. This empirical line of inquiry reinforced the principle that transit decarbonization strategies cannot rely on one-size-fits-all mandates, but must instead be tailored to the specific topological and operational profiles of individual transit networks.

## Dynamic Evaluation Frameworks and Real Options Valuation Under Market Volatility

To address the limitations of static life-cycle modeling when evaluating fleet transitions under severe volatility, Professor Giagnorio introduced dynamic valuation models incorporating macroeconomic and technological uncertainties. In 'Investments in local public transport: A systematic literature review on evaluation methods with a focus on the role of uncertainty' (2025), he systematically mapped the methodological evolution of transit investment appraisal. The review critiqued the sector's reliance on static discounted cash flow and deterministic cost-benefit analyses, emphasizing the necessity of incorporating Monte Carlo simulations and Real Options Analysis to capture volatility in energy prices, asset residual values, and rapid technological advancements.

Operationalizing this theoretical shift, Professor Giagnorio formulated an advanced valuation framework in 'Bus fleet decarbonization under macroeconomic and technological uncertainties: A real options approach to support decision-making' (2024). Utilizing a multi-option least squares Monte Carlo simulation, the model evaluated multi-stage replacement pathways across diesel, hybrid, compressed natural gas, and electric powertrains under fluctuating electricity prices, battery decay functions, and salvage values. Applied across inner-city, urban, suburban, and commuter routes, the real options approach demonstrated potential total cost of ownership savings of up to ten percent compared to static decision rules. The study highlighted that while inner-city routes favor early electrification, short concession contract durations without regulatory residual value guarantees significantly stifle operator willingness to invest in capital-intensive zero-emission fleets.

## Interpretable Machine Learning for Public Transport Cost Prediction

Complementing structural engineering and real options frameworks, Professor Giagnorio integrated advanced predictive analytics into transport economics to enhance the precision of expenditure forecasting in public concessions. In 'Predicting costs of local public bus transport services through machine learning methods' (2025), he developed machine learning algorithms trained on a comprehensive dataset of 269 US transit agencies operating between 2015 and 2019 to predict efficient total economic costs per vehicle revenue-mile.

By deploying non-linear machine learning architectures alongside SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) feature attribution techniques, the research demonstrated superior predictive accuracy and robustness compared to traditional linear regression models. The feature selection strategy identified commercial speed, employee wage structures, vehicle productivity, and fleet ownership costs as the primary drivers of unit operating expenditure. The interpretability offered by SHAP value analysis enables public transport authorities to simulate the cost impacts of specific contract modifications, establishing a highly objective basis for setting price caps, allocating national subsidy funds, and defining maximum economic compensation in competitive concession tenders.

## Multi-Dimensional Taxonomies for Large-Scale Zero-Emission Bus Diffusion

Moving from micro-level cost predictions to macro-level institutional dynamics, Professor Giagnorio investigated the managerial, social, and policy barriers that govern the scaling of clean transit technologies. In 'Factors influencing the adoption of zero-emission buses: A review-based framework' (2024), he synthesized an extensive body of international literature into a holistic taxonomy and hierarchical decision framework governing the transition from small-scale pilot projects to large-scale zero-emission bus fleet deployment.

This multi-dimensional analysis revealed a fundamental structural divergence in fleet adoption drivers: while technological complexities, capital constraints, and managerial uncertainty historically hampered large-scale deployment, institutional mandates, environmental target-setting, and social acceptance served as primary catalysts. The framework systematically documented how recent advances in battery management systems and fleet dispatching software are mitigating traditional operational drawbacks. By mapping these systemic drivers, the study provided transit agencies and policymakers with an integrative roadmap to harmonize technological readiness with organizational capacity and public funding mechanisms.

## Market Structure, Vertical Integration, and Regulation in Charging Networks

Extending his decarbonization research into the broader electric mobility ecosystem, Professor Giagnorio applied microeconomic industrial organization theory to analyze electric vehicle charging infrastructure markets. In 'Vertical integration, interoperability, and subsidies in electric vehicle charging markets' (2026), he constructed a formal theoretical model governing the market interactions between Charging Point Operators (CPOs) and Mobility Service Providers (MSPs), incorporating indirect network externalities between charging density and consumer EV adoption.

The theoretical model and subsequent empirical calibration to Italian metropolitan areas resolved key policy debates regarding market structure and regulatory interventions. Professor Giagnorio demonstrated that while vertical integration between CPOs and MSPs introduces risks of downstream competitor foreclosure, integrated firms internalize network complementarities, yielding higher infrastructure investment, greater charging density, and superior total social welfare compared to vertically unbundled markets. Furthermore, the analysis evaluated regulatory counter-measures, showing that minimum-margin rules soften foreclosure without eliminating investment incentives, whereas compliance costs associated with strict interoperability standards can inadvertently suppress network expansion. The study also highlighted a vital fiscal trade-off: direct consumer subsidies yield greater aggregate social welfare gains, whereas firm-directed capital subsidies achieve higher carbon reduction efficiency per public euro spent.

## Discrete Choice Analysis and Perceived Value of Clean Transit Operations

Complementing supply-side infrastructure and cost modeling, Professor Giagnorio investigated the demand-side consumer dynamics that determine public acceptance and modal shift toward electrified public transit. In 'Early consumer response to electric buses in urban areas: Evidence from a discrete choice experiment' (2026), he conducted an empirical web-based discrete choice experiment in Rome, estimating a hybrid choice model embedded with latent variables for technology awareness, perceived environmental benefits, and social norms.

The empirical findings established that urban commuters perceive and assign quantifiable economic value to the operational attributes of electric buses, specifically quieter operation, smoother acceleration, and reduced localized pollution at bus stops. However, the study revealed that technology valuation is strongly mediated by prior familiarity and environmental sensitivity. Crucially, the choice model demonstrated that the perceived comfort gains of electric buses primarily enhance satisfaction among existing transit riders rather than driving significant modal shift from private automobiles, as private car users exhibited lower sensitivity to environmental and social norms. These insights provide transport planners with an empirical foundation for designing targeted marketing campaigns, public outreach strategies, and service quality benchmarks that maximize the societal return on transit electrification investments.
