## The Microeconomics of Transport Transition: Economic Modeling under Technological Volatility

Professor Mirko Giagnorio has constructed a unified research program situated at the intersection of transport economics, industrial organization, operations research, and econometric modeling. Central to his scholarly inquiry is the challenge of guiding public transport governance and capital allocation through the ecological and digital transitions. Rather than treating transit technology adoption as a static equipment replacement decision, Giagnorio investigates the complex interplay between cost structures, technological uncertainty, market design, regulatory mechanisms, and passenger behavior. His work bridges high-level welfare economics with granular, engineering-grounded cost accounting to establish actionable frameworks for transit authorities and private operators.

At the core of Giagnorio's agenda is a methodological commitment to replacing deterministic, static appraisal frameworks with dynamic, stochastic, and structural models. By modeling the operational, spatial, and market realities of transit systems—ranging from regional rail and urban metros to zero-emission bus fleets and public charging networks—his research demonstrates how regulatory parameters, contract structures, and operational decisions directly condition the economic viability and environmental performance of sustainable transport infrastructure.

## Parametric Engineering-Economic Cost Benchmarking and Regulatory Compensation

To establish rigorous baseline standards for transit regulation and auction design, Giagnorio developed a multi-layered methodological suite for cost benchmarking across urban rail modes. In 'A proxy cost model for tramway services' (2020), he established a disaggregated proxy cost framework to separate transport service production, infrastructure, maintenance, administrative, and capital costs for Italian tramways, enabling transit authorities to compute optimal auction bases and compensation ceilings based on operator-controlled productivities and authority-controlled variables such as assigned revenue-kilometers and fleet age. Expanding this engineering-economic perspective to heavy regional transit, 'A bottom-up cost model for electric railbased regional public transport services: The ITALIAN CONTEXT' (2021) constructed a bottom-up process model relying on technical production analysis across four operational expense categories, removing historical operator inefficiencies and revealing commercial speed and service frequency as the primary determinants of unit standard costs.

Recognizing that modal technologies differ inherently in capital intensity and operational mechanics, Giagnorio advanced from pure proxy and bottom-up models to hybrid frameworks. In 'A hybrid cost model for light and heavy metro services' (2025), he integrated degree of automation, wheel technology, and peak hourly capacity into a unified standard cost model using the complete universe of Italian metro revenue-kilometers. This work demonstrated that heavy metro services, while requiring substantially higher initial capital outlay and train depreciation, achieve superior cost efficiency on a per seat-kilometer basis due to massive capacity, mathematically justifying large-scale capital investments only when peak demand thresholds are satisfied.

To complement parametric cost structures with advanced predictive tools, Giagnorio incorporated non-parametric machine learning into regulatory econometrics. In 'Predicting costs of local public bus transport services through machine learning methods' (2025), he trained predictive algorithms on a multi-year panel of two hundred and sixty-nine US transit agencies, combining supervised learning with SHAP feature attribution to uncover non-linear cost drivers such as commercial speed, vehicle productivity, and employee compensation. This synthesis of engineering cost modeling and interpretable machine learning provides regulatory authorities with robust benchmarks for concession contract design and public resource allocation.

## Stochastic Valuation, Real Options, and Fleet Transition under Volatility

Transitioning from baseline cost benchmarking to strategic decision-making under uncertainty, Giagnorio addressed the limitations of traditional transit appraisal. In 'Investments in local public transport: A systematic literature review on evaluation methods with a focus on the role of uncertainty' (2025), he systematically synthesized the evaluation landscape across discounted cash flows, total cost of ownership, and cost-benefit analysis, demonstrating that despite systemic macroeconomic and technological shifts, the literature remains dominated by deterministic frameworks that obscure volatility. To overcome these limitations, Giagnorio developed empirical life-cycle models in 'Assessing cost-effectiveness of alternative bus technologies: Evidence from US transit agencies' (2023), evaluating active fleets across two hundred and fifty-six US transit operators. This empirical life-cycle accounting revealed that full-electric buses achieve cost-competitiveness primarily in dense, highly congested metropolitan environments characterized by high service frequency and elevated marginal impacts of local emissions, whereas longer, higher-speed suburban routes continue to face severe technical and economic barriers.

Building upon empirical life-cycle structures, Giagnorio introduced advanced real options analysis to capture managerial flexibility under stochastic fuel, electricity, battery, and salvage value dynamics. In 'Bus fleet decarbonization under macroeconomic and technological uncertainties: A real options approach to support decision-making' (2024), he deployed a multi-option least-squares Monte Carlo real options framework to compute dynamic, route-specific fleet replacement trajectories across diesel, hybrid, compressed natural gas, and full-electric powertrains. The analysis demonstrated that while internal combustion engines remain cost-effective in early stages, electric transition trajectories optimal for inner-city routes accelerate over time, while short concession contract durations lacking fleet residual value guarantees severely disincentivize private operator investment in zero-emission technology.

## Welfare Optimization, Network Externalities, and Market Structure in Electrification

While real options models address private operator decision-making, systemic electrification requires aligning operator incentives with public welfare, pricing, and infrastructure market design. In 'Introducing electric buses in urban areas: Effects on welfare, pricing, frequency, and public subsidies' (2024), Giagnorio linked microeconomic fleet cost accounting with a joint welfare-optimization model governing transit fares, service frequencies, and road pricing for high-frequency urban corridors. Analyzing depot and opportunity charging configurations, he established that low external emission savings do not automatically offset high capital expenditures for batteries and charging infrastructure, proving that optimizing operational efficiency—such as routing, driving style, and climate control systems—yields cost and welfare improvements that rival pure battery technology cost reductions.

Extending his analysis from public transit fleets to the broader microeconomics of electric mobility infrastructure, Giagnorio examined the industrial organization of public charging markets. In 'Vertical integration, interoperability, and subsidies in electric vehicle charging markets' (2026), he constructed a theoretical model of two-sided market interactions between Charging Point Operators and Mobility Service Providers, incorporating indirect network externalities between charging density and vehicle adoption. The study demonstrated that vertically integrated firms internalize infrastructure-adoption complementarities to expand network density and overall social welfare, even under market foreclosure conditions. Furthermore, the work evaluated minimum-margin interoperability regulations and contrasted firm-side and consumer-side subsidies, proving through empirical calibration that consumer subsidies generate larger social welfare gains while firm-side subsidies deliver higher environmental abatement per fiscal euro spent.

## Behavioral Demand Heterogeneity and Socio-Technical Barriers to Adoption

To complete the transition architecture, Giagnorio linked macroeconomic and organizational supply models with demand-side behavioral economics and socio-technical transition theory. In 'Factors influencing the adoption of zero-emission buses: A review-based framework' (2024), he synthesized the multi-dimensional barriers obstructing large-scale zero-emission bus deployment, constructing a taxonomic framework that categorizes technological, managerial, social, environmental, and institutional determinants. The review highlighted that while structural and financial constraints historically impeded diffusion, recent technological and operational advances are systematically mitigating adoption drawbacks.

On the empirical demand side, Giagnorio investigated how service qualitative attributes drive passenger modal choices beyond traditional time and cost trade-offs. In 'Early consumer response to electric buses in urban areas: Evidence from a discrete choice experiment' (2026), he combined discrete choice experiments with a structural hybrid choice model incorporating latent psychometric constructs such as technology awareness, perceived environmental benefits, and social norms. The empirical findings demonstrated that while existing transit riders place significant value on the smoother, quieter, and lower-emission attributes of electric buses, private car drivers display low sensitivity to social norms and environmental advantages. This structural insight indicates that bus electrification primarily enhances service quality for current riders rather than triggering immediate modal shift, underscoring the necessity of combining fleet electrification with targeted policy interventions.
